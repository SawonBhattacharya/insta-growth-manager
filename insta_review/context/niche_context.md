## Niche definition

**Core niche:** Middle-class Indian roof cricket and gully cricket nostalgia — the lived cricket
culture of urban Indians who grew up in apartments, chawls, and colonies where there was no
backyard, only a rooftop, a narrow lane, or a building compound.

**This is NOT:**
- Professional cricket commentary or analysis
- IPL news, match updates, or score reactions
- Stadium culture or fan club content
- Generic "cricket is life" motivational content
- Backyard cricket (that is the Australian/English version, not the Indian middle-class version)

**This IS:**
- The specific, hyper-relatable world of cricket played in constrained urban Indian spaces
- The unwritten rules, characters, arguments, and rituals every Indian city kid recognises
- The emotional memory of a childhood spent negotiating slipper-wickets and water-tank sixes
- A cultural archive of middle-class Indian cricket life before turf pitches and leather balls

## The roof cricket universe — specific cultural markers

Every piece of content should reference at least one of these. These details are what make
content feel genuinely remembered rather than generically imagined.

**Physical landmarks:**
- Rooftop playing surface (rough concrete, slightly sloped, cracks that spin the ball)
- Water tank (six over it = legendary; ball behind it = 10-minute argument)
- Parapet walls (boundary or automatic out — depends which building you grew up in)
- Neighbour's terrace (ball lands there = trespass negotiation, batsman always sent)
- Staircase door (fielding position and emergency exit simultaneously)
- Chalk stumps drawn on the wall
- The downpipe that always deflected the ball at an unplayable angle

**Equipment:**
- Tennis ball (new = fast; old = reverse swing machine)
- Tape ball (heavier, swings more, stings more)
- Rubber ball (the original, smells like 2003)
- Plastic bat (always cracking at the shoulder, always someone's fault)
- Borrowed bat (owner bats at 1, always, no vote required)
- Slipper wickets (standing flat slippers, argued about all match)

**Unwritten rules (universal across every Indian city):**
- One-tip-one-hand = out. No debate. Ever.
- Ball in the neighbour's house = automatic out. Batsman retrieves it.
- Owner of the ball bats first. This is not discussed.
- Last over before mom calls = highest-pressure cricket on earth.
- Disputed LBW with a tennis ball = never resolved, always remembered.
- Six off the water tank = the shot that gets retold for years.
- Rain interruption = DLS decided by whoever argues loudest.
- Ball on the roof below = game suspended, recovery mission begins.

**Characters (every para had every single one of these):**
- The boy who owned the ball (more power than the captain, used it freely)
- The boy who owned the bat (opened every innings, no discussion)
- The boy who never got out (invented excuses; eventually the group just accepted it)
- The fast bowler who hurt everyone but never took wickets
- The mystery spinner who turned it on concrete (no one understood how)
- The captain who lost every toss (blamed the coin, every time)
- The mental scoreboard keeper (perfectly accurate, always disputed)
- The one elite fielder everyone fought to have on their side

**Emotional situations:**
- Mom calling from 3 floors down — match abandoned mid-delivery
- First ball that hit the window — entire building went silent
- The match that lasted 3 days across three separate evenings
- The six that cleared the parapet and was never recovered
- Being given out LBW to a tennis ball and knowing, just knowing, it was missing leg
- The argument that technically never ended and still hasn't

## Six content pillars (in strict priority order)

### Pillar 1 — Rules of the Roof (post immediately, highest priority)
Recurring series. Each episode covers one unwritten rule every Indian city kid followed without
being told. Format: state the rule, show the situation it applied to, show the argument it always
caused. Hook names the specific rule in the first 1.5 seconds. Designed to be forwarded to
school WhatsApp groups.

### Pillar 2 — That one player every para had (post immediately)
Recurring series. Each episode introduces one universal archetype from every neighbourhood
cricket game. The ball owner. The one who never got out. The mystery spinner. Format: introduce
the archetype with a single line, show the situation, end with a line every viewer recognises from
their own childhood.

### Pillar 3 — The match that never ended (post from week 3 onwards)
Story-driven reels. Reconstruct a specific type of match situation that always ended the same
way — mom calling, rain, a broken window, the argument that could never be settled. Format:
set the scene, build the tension, land the interruption everyone in the audience has lived.

### Pillar 4 — Fan emotion / relatable cricket fan POV (1 in every 4 reels maximum)
The working adult watching IPL at 10 PM after a day of work calls. The "AI Consultant by day,
cricket fan forever" energy. Connects roof cricket nostalgia to present-day fan identity. Used
sparingly to anchor the page in current relevance without drifting into generic cricket content.

### Pillar 5 — Roof vs turf evolution (build after 200 followers)
Bridge content: the same people who played on rooftops now play on turf. Same people, same
spirit, upgraded ground. Introduces community cricket storytelling. Do not post this pillar before
the core nostalgia community is established.

### Pillar 6 — AI + cricket crossover (build after 500 followers)
The unique lane no other cricket creator can own. "I asked an AI to pick India's XI using only
gully cricket logic." "AI analyses the unwritten rules of roof cricket." Comedy plus professional
identity. Deploy only after the nostalgia community is well established — premature use dilutes
the niche identity.

## Visual language

- Aesthetic: lo-fi, slightly underexposed, digi-cam feel — NOT cinematic, NOT polished brand video
- Light: golden hour or late afternoon on rooftops — the quality of light this cricket was actually played in
- Locations: real Kolkata rooftops, lanes, building compounds — not staged or studio-looking
- On-screen text: bold, slightly imperfect font — works for silent viewing, reads immediately
- Colour: warm, slightly desaturated — the look of a 2005 phone camera
- Props: slipper wickets, rubber ball, chalk on wall, water tank visible in background — at least one per video
- Avoid: slow-motion cinematic shots, green screen, heavy effects, stock footage, anything that looks like a brand ad

## Brand voice

- Warm and knowing — like someone who was there, remembers exactly, and is genuinely happy to share
- Slightly wry — there is always a gentle joke in the delivery, never forced
- Never preachy or motivational — zero "cricket taught me life lessons" energy
- Specific over general — "the boy who owned the ball always opened the batting" beats "cricket was everything"
- Conversational — captions written like a WhatsApp message to a childhood friend, not a creator caption
- Authentic — occasional Bangla or Hindi phrase where it fits naturally, never forced
- AI Consultant layer — exists as a quiet differentiator, only surfaces in Pillar 6

## Caption formula

Line 1: The specific hook — a situation or rule that triggers the memory. Must work as a
standalone WhatsApp forward, no context needed.

"Tell me the rule in your lane."

Example:
  The boy who owned the ball always batted at number 1. No vote. No discussion. Just the rule.
  Tag him. He knows who he is.

## Audience profile

Primary: Urban Indian men and women, 25–40, grew up middle-class in tier-1 or tier-2 cities,
played street or roof cricket as children, now have corporate or professional jobs. Respond to
content that says "I see your specific childhood, not a generic Indian childhood."

Secondary: Indian diaspora abroad, 25–45, same roof/gully cricket memories, heightened
nostalgia response as a connection to home.

Engagement behaviour: These users do not just like. They tag childhood friends, forward to
school WhatsApp groups, and leave full-sentence comments ("bhai this is exactly our building
in 2003, even the water tank placement is the same"). That comment pattern is the community
signal — it means a genuine memory was triggered.
